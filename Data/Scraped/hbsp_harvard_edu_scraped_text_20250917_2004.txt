COURSE DESIGN
6 Tips for Better Lesson Planning
A Crash Course in Instructional Design
by Sophie Oberstein
August 28, 2025
Boris SV / Getty Images

Course Design
Interactive Learning

F
aculty are experts in their content areas, but that doesn’t necessarily mean they’re experts in designing effective learning experiences. While some institutions offer instructional design teams to help faculty who may lack experience in this area, this opportunity isn’t offered consistently across higher education.
Fortunately, there are several straightforward frameworks that can help you plan and structure course content. These models not only help with organization, they also tap into essential principles for how students absorb, retain, and apply new information. Even for educators already familiar with these models, revisiting their core elements can be a valuable way to keep teaching practices effective and well-grounded.
In the classes I teach for NYU’s Certificate in Learning Design program, I introduce some of the more widely used frameworks, including
Gagne’s Nine Events of Instruction
,
ROPES
(Relate-Overview-Presentation-Exercise-Summary),
Kolb’s Experiential Learning Cycle
, and
NeuroLeadership Institute’s
AGES
(Attention-Generation-Emotion-Spacing). These models attempt to capture the key elements of an effective class session or syllabus. While there are differences among them, all are grounded in learning science and share common components that support student success.
While you don’t need to master every framework, becoming familiar with their commonalities can help you design more effective, engaging instruction. Here are six themes you can implement into your course design that will allow you to reap the benefits of multiple models without further study of instructional design.
1. Focus students’ attention
The
hippocampus
, the part of the brain that makes sense of new information, can be distracted when our minds wander. So if we want our students to learn effectively, we need to hold their attention.
Both the AGES and Gagne models emphasize this, but from slightly different angles. AGES highlights the importance of focusing learners’ attention on one subject, without distractions, while Gagne’s stresses the need to grab learners’ attention right off the bat so they’re eager to learn more. The overall idea of both models is that we need to help students create mental space for focused learning.
“Think like a beginner. To know what’s truly essential, remember what it felt like to learn this material for the first time.”
One way to do this is to help learners eliminate distractions. You might try beginning class each day by inviting students to clear their minds. This could be as simple as asking them to close their eyes and take a deep breath, or having them each answer a question like “What do you need to put aside to participate fully today?” Let them know that whatever is occupying their brain space will still be there when class is over.
Once learners are present, you’ll want to hook them in to the day’s topic right away. A provocative statement or headline, a rhetorical question, an interesting statistic, a demonstration, or a quick self-assessment can all help create that initial spark.
For instance, in a session on designing for neurodivergent learners, I might start with a statistic such as, “Recent studies identify nearly 30 percent of the population as neurodivergent.” Or, in a lesson on the forgetting curve, I might start with a reflection question, such as “How many of you have spent hours in trainings learning something new and struggled to recall the information just one day later?”
These
opening moments
signal to the brain, “Pay attention.”
2. Relate content to what they already know
Several learning design models emphasize the importance of moving information into students’ long-term memory by connecting it to already established neural pathways. (For example, the R in ROPES stands for “Review” or “Relate.”) This helps them make sense of new information more quickly and creates deeper pathways for accessing knowledge later. This is known as
constructivism
.
The simplest way to spark these connections is to start new modules with questions like “What do you already know about . . .” or “How have you experienced . . .” This identifies existing pathways for new content to travel into long-term memory. It’s like an encoding shortcut so that learners can attach new information to something that already makes sense to them.
Mnemonic devices are another effective tool because they link new knowledge to something familiar. For example, the French word for “door” is
porte
. Instead of trying to get students to memorize that through repetition (“
porte
means door;
porte
means door”), cue learners to think about how
porte
sounds like “portal,” and how a portal is an entryway in English.
These kinds of connections—whether sparked by questions or memory cues—can be intentionally structured in four ways:
Content to content:
linking new material to previous lessons or topics students have learned about in the past
Content to text:
connecting ideas to readings or having students make those connections in small group discussions
Content to self:
encouraging learners to relate concepts to their personal experiences
Content to world:
asking learners to connect content to current events and the larger world
By intentionally weaving these strategies together, instructors deepen comprehension, strengthen recall, and build the neural pathways that make learning last.
3. Engage them emotionally
Relating new content to what learners already know is powerful, but how can we create new connections to content where no pathways exist yet? This is where the E (“Emotions”) in AGES can help. Emotionally charged experiences stick in memory far more than neutral ones.
Think about your own educational experiences. Which classes do you remember best: those with interesting facts, or those that made you feel something?
For me, it’s the latter. Decades ago, I took a course called Education in the Nuclear Age. On the first day, instead of reciting statistics on nuclear stockpiles, the instructor dropped a single metal pellet into a bowl. That pellet, he said, represented how much nuclear weaponry was used during the bombing of Hiroshima. Then, he poured a bucket of pellets slowly into the dish. That thunderous sound represented the world’s current nuclear arsenal. I can still remember the rush of emotions I felt.
“Storytelling is a great way to weave emotion into your instruction. In addition to your own stories, you can glean narratives from guest speakers, TV, podcasts, or from the learners themselves.”
Do you have a similar moment from your learning that stands out? If so, it’s likely because it triggered some feelings.
Facts without emotion
rarely stick
. For example, “The Queen died, and then the King died,” is a historical fact. But if you add sentiment, “The Queen died, and the King died of grief,” suddenly it becomes a story you remember.
Storytelling is a great way to weave emotion into your instruction. In addition to your own stories, you can glean narratives from guest speakers, TV, podcasts, or from the learners themselves. Simulations, case studies, dramatic demonstrations, music, and art all carry emotional weight.
4. Don’t cram in too much content
As educators, we want to share all the knowledge we have, but we need to do a better job of distinguishing what’s a “nice to know” from what’s a “need to know” to prevent cognitive overload for our students. This means taking a hard look at your content and identifying what’s essential for mastering the course concepts, while moving the rest to optional readings or reference materials.
As an instructional designer, this is the advice I give subject matter experts most often: Think like a beginner. To know what’s truly essential, remember what it felt like to learn this material for the first time.
Course structure also matters. Shorter, more frequent sessions, where learners have time to absorb and retrieve information, are ideal. This is where the S (“Spacing”) in the
AGES framework
comes into play. If a longer session is necessary, break the content into digestible chunks.
5. Leave time for practice and application
Avoiding cognitive overload is only part of the equation. Learners also need time to practice and apply what they’ve learned. In many learning models, practice (also referred to as “exercise”) is allotted the most class time. For example, the ROPES model recommends dedicating 35–50 percent of a session to practice, while Kolb’s framework has learners applying everything they’ve learned in a culminating experience they’ve designed.
“Practice without feedback is not just ineffective, it can be harmful. If students practice something incorrectly, they learn to do it the wrong way.”
Without rehearsal, new information never makes it into long-term memory and is forgotten. Practice is your main engagement and retention tool. As Confucius said, “I hear and I forget. I see and I remember. I do and I understand.”
That “doing” can take many forms: teach-backs (where individuals or groups teach what they’ve learned to someone else),
simulations
, application exercises (like spotting critical-thinking principles in advertising or trying a new painting technique in an art class), or, if the course is skills-based, performing that skill under various conditions.
6. Provide feedback after practice
Many of these models say practice alone isn’t enough. Practice without feedback is not just ineffective, it can be harmful. If students practice something incorrectly, they learn to do it the wrong way.
Consider final exams. In my NYU courses, I conduct the final exam before the last class, so we can review the answers together. Some of my adult learners have told me that this is the first time in their academic careers they’ve had this experience; often, college exams are due on the last day of class or during an exam period. Once students hand in the blue book with their responses, they never see it again. What is the point of having students turn in a final exam if they don’t get feedback beyond a grade?
Feedback should confirm what’s correct and correct what’s wrong to ensure that learners take away the right messages. For subjects in which there aren’t necessarily correct answers, feedback should acknowledge the depth and breadth of possible responses. Ask more than one or two students to share their answers to get a feel for the diversity of perspectives.
Feedback can also be provided on the process: How did students approach the assignment or question? Did learners use their time well? Did they collaborate well? Did they have the tools they needed, and, if they didn’t, did they ask for them? By commenting on the process, learners of all subjects develop their critical-thinking skills, a key competency in today’s world.
Finally,
don’t underestimate peer feedback
. It can be just as powerful as the instructor’s take, if learners are equipped to share honest, thoughtful input, and to hear and integrate it. Set aside some time in initial sessions to teach them best practices for doing so.
Transforming information sharing into understanding
We can no longer rely on the richness of our subject matter alone to engage learners. Information is abundant; what’s scarce is meaningful instruction that helps learners connect, remember, and apply it.
Integrating proven principles of adult learning and common threads from key instructional models can bridge that gap and lead to deep, lasting understanding.
